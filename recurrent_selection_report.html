1. Introduction

Recurrent selection is a cyclical plant breeding procedure designed to progressively increase the frequency of favorable alleles in a population while maintaining sufficient genetic variability for continued improvement. Unlike line or hybrid breeding, which ultimately fix genotypes, recurrent selection treats the population itself as the product—continuously refined through repeated cycles of evaluation and intermating.

Core Principle: Select the best individuals (or families) from a population, intercross them to produce a new base population, and repeat—each cycle shifting allele frequencies toward the desired direction.

The method was formally conceptualized in the mid-20th century primarily for cross-pollinated (allogamous) crops, but has since been extended to self-pollinated (autogamous) species and even vegetatively propagated crops. It is particularly powerful for:

  • Quantitative traits controlled by many genes of small effect (polygenic traits)
  • Improving general combining ability (GCA) and specific combining ability (SCA)
  • Broadening the genetic base of elite breeding populations
  • Population improvement before variety or hybrid development

2. Historical Background

The intellectual roots of recurrent selection trace to early 20th-century genetics and the realization that simple mass selection in cross-pollinated species suffered from diminishing returns.

The Illinois Long-Term Selection Experiment (started 1896, still ongoing) stands as the most compelling empirical proof: after 100+ generations of recurrent selection, maize grain oil content has risen from ~5% to >20% (high-oil line) and dropped to <1% (low-oil line), demonstrating the near-inexhaustible reservoir of additive genetic variance in large populations.

3. Objectives & Rationale

Primary Objectives

  • Increase mean performance of the population for target traits
  • Maintain or enhance genetic diversity
  • Improve general and/or specific combining ability
  • Provide superior parent material for variety or hybrid development

Why Not Simple Mass Selection?

  • No recombination step → favorable alleles not reshuffled
  • Rapid loss of heterozygosity in cross-pollinators
  • Cannot exploit epistasis or dominance interactions
  • Progress plateaus quickly due to linkage disequilibrium
Key Insight: Each cycle of recurrent selection increases the frequency of desirable alleles while the intercrossing step breaks unfavorable linkages, generating new combinations for the next cycle to act on.

4. Genetic Basis

4.1 Types of Gene Action

The efficiency of different recurrent selection schemes depends strongly on the predominant gene action underlying the target trait:

Gene ActionDescriptionBest RS Scheme
Additive (A)Alleles add linearly to phenotype; exploited by all selection methodsAny RS, especially phenotypic & half-sib
Dominance (D)Heterozygote advantage; important for hybrid vigorFull-sib RS, RRS
Epistasis (I)Interaction between loci; difficult to exploitRRS, genomic-aided RS
Additive × Additive (AA)Can be fixed; heritableFull-sib or S₁ family RS

4.2 Genetic Variance Components

The total phenotypic variance (VP) is partitioned as:

VP = VA + VD + VI + VGE + VE

// Where:
// VA = Additive genetic variance (narrow-sense heritability)
// VD = Dominance variance
// VI = Epistatic variance
// VGE = Genotype × Environment interaction variance
// VE = Environmental variance

4.3 Selection Response

Per-cycle genetic gain (ΔG) is given by the Breeder's Equation:

ΔG = i × h² × σP

// i = Selection intensity (standardized selection differential)
// h² = Narrow-sense heritability (VA / VP)
// σP = Phenotypic standard deviation

For family-based RS schemes, the response equation is modified to account for the within-family and between-family variance components, and the selection unit (individual, half-sib family, full-sib family, or selfed progeny).

4.4 Effective Population Size (Ne)

Critical Consideration: Genetic drift becomes a serious concern when Ne is small. A minimum of 50–100 selected parents per cycle is recommended to avoid inbreeding depression and allele loss. Ne ≈ 4NmNf / (Nm + Nf) for unequal sex ratios.

5. Types of Recurrent Selection

Six major categories of recurrent selection have been developed, each differing in the selection unit, the tester used, and the gene action exploited:

① Phenotypic Recurrent Selection (PRS)

Selection based solely on the phenotypic performance of individuals in the base population. Simple, fast, and effective for high-heritability traits. Exploits mainly additive variance.

② Half-Sib Recurrent Selection (HSRS)

Progenies from open-pollinated (or specific tester-pollinated) families are evaluated. Selection among and within half-sib families. Exploits additive genetic variance; widely used in maize.

③ Full-Sib Recurrent Selection (FSRS)

Full-sib families are created by controlled crosses; remnant seed of selected families is used for recombination. Exploits additive and part of dominance variance; more expensive but faster per cycle genetic gain.

④ S₁ (Selfed-Progeny) Recurrent Selection

Plants are selfed one generation to produce S₁ families, which are evaluated and the remnant seed of the best families intercrossed. Particularly effective in self-pollinated species; exposes recessive alleles.

⑤ Recurrent Selection for GCA (RSGCA)

Uses a broad-genetic-base tester (inbred or synthetic) to measure general combining ability. Improves mean hybrid performance across many cross combinations. Proposed by Sprague & Tatum.

⑥ Reciprocal Recurrent Selection (RRS)

Two complementary populations (A & B) are simultaneously improved, each acting as the tester for the other. Improves SCA between populations. Ideal for hybrid programs. Introduced by Comstock, Robinson & Harvey (1952).

Variants also include: Recurrent Selection for SCA (RSSCA), Modified Ear-to-Row selection, Grid selection, and Marker-Assisted Recurrent Selection (MARS).

6. General Procedure (One Cycle)

Although details vary by scheme, all recurrent selection methods share a common four-phase cycle:

  1. 1

    Establishment of the Base Population (C₀)

    A diverse base population is assembled by intercrossing multiple adapted or exotic germplasm sources. Genetic diversity is the raw material; wider crosses increase VA but may reduce mean performance. A minimum of 200–300 plants is recommended for C₀.

  2. 2

    Development of Selection Units

    Depending on the scheme, individual plants are selfed (S₁ families), open-pollinated (half-sib families), or crossed in pairs (full-sib families). Sufficient seed is stored as remnant seed for later use in recombination.

  3. 3

    Progeny Testing / Evaluation

    Families or individual progenies are grown in replicated, multi-environment trials (METs). Appropriate experimental designs (RCBD, alpha-lattice, augmented designs) and statistical models (mixed models, BLUP) are used to obtain best linear unbiased predictors of family/genotype performance.

  4. 4

    Selection

    Top-performing families or individuals (typically 10–20% selection intensity) are identified. The selection criterion may be a single trait or a selection index combining multiple traits.

  5. 5

    Recombination (Intermating)

    Remnant seed of the selected units is grown and allowed (or controlled) to intermate freely. This restores heterozygosity, breaks linkage disequilibrium, and generates the new base population (C₁).

  6. 6

    Repeat

    Cycles C₁ → C₂ → C₃ … continue until the population mean reaches the breeding target or the rate of gain diminishes. Superior families at any cycle may also be advanced for line/hybrid development in parallel.

7. Methods in Detail

7.1 Phenotypic Recurrent Selection

Procedure: (1) Grow base population → (2) Rogue inferior plants before anthesis → (3) Allow cross-pollination among superior plants → (4) Harvest seed to form C₁.

Cycle length: 1 season (in annual crops) — the fastest scheme.
Limitations: Low heritability traits respond poorly; no control of pollen parent; confounded by G×E.

7.2 Half-Sib Recurrent Selection

Procedure:

  1. Year 1, Season 1: Grow base pop. Open-pollinate (or pollinate with a broad tester) each selected plant → collect HS family seed + save remnant pollen/seed.
  2. Year 1, Season 2 / Year 2: Evaluate HS families in replicated trials.
  3. Select best families based on progeny performance.
  4. Year 2–3: Grow remnant seed of selected families → intermate → form C₁.

Cycle length: 2–3 seasons.
Advantages: Removes environmental confounding; effective for moderately heritable traits.

7.3 Full-Sib Recurrent Selection

Full-sib families are produced by controlled pair crosses. Because both parents are known, the selection unit captures more genetic information. This method exploits both additive variance and part of dominance variance.

Expected genetic gain per cycle: Typically 20–30% greater than half-sib RS for the same trait, at the cost of more controlled pollinations.

7.4 S₁ (Selfed-Progeny) Recurrent Selection

Plants are selfed to produce S₁ progenies. These are evaluated in replicated trials and the best S₁ families are intercrossed (using remnant S₀ seed) to form C₁.

Special Value: Selfing exposes deleterious recessive alleles, allowing negative selection against them. This leads to faster improvement of inbred-line performance and is increasingly used in self-pollinated crops (wheat, soybean, rice).

7.5 Recurrent Selection for GCA

A tester with a broad genetic base (often a synthetic variety or open-pollinated variety) is used to evaluate GCA. Each plant in the base population is crossed to the tester, and progeny performance in test crosses guides selection.

GCA = mean performance of all cross-combinations of a genotype with all other genotypes in the population. The tester should be genetically broad (to test GCA) or narrow (to test SCA).

7.6 Reciprocal Recurrent Selection (RRS)

Two heterotic populations (A and B) are simultaneously improved:

  1. Each plant in Population A is crossed to a plant in Population B (and vice versa).
  2. Progeny (A × B crosses) are evaluated in replicated trials.
  3. Plants in A are selected based on performance of A × B crosses; same for B.
  4. Selected plants within A are intermated → new C₁A; same for B → C₁B.
  5. Next cycle: C₁A plants crossed to C₁B plants, evaluated, and selected.
Advantage of RRS: Simultaneously improves both populations for between- population performance (SCA), maximizing heterosis in A×B hybrids while still improving each population independently.

8. Key Formulae

8.1 Breeder's Equation (Genetic Gain)

ΔG = (i · rAI · σA) / L

// i = Selection intensity (from standard normal tables)
// rAI = Accuracy of selection (correlation of index with true breeding value)
// σA = Additive genetic standard deviation
// L = Cycle length (years)

8.2 Heritability (Narrow-Sense)

h² = VA / VP     // range 0–1; higher → greater response to selection

// For half-sib families:
h²HS = 4σ²HS / (σ²HS + σ²w/n + σ²e/rn)

8.3 Selection Differential & Intensity

S = X̄selected − X̄original     // Selection differential

i = S / σP     // Standardized selection intensity; i ≈ 1.76 for top 10% selected

8.4 Inbreeding Coefficient After t Cycles of Selfing

Ft = 1 − (1/2)t     // F → 1 asymptotically; recombination step keeps F low in RS

8.5 GCA & SCA (ANOVA Approach)

GCAi = X̄i· − X̄··
SCAij = X̄ij − X̄i· − X̄·j + X̄··

// X̄i· = mean of all crosses involving parent i
// X̄·· = grand mean of all crosses
// X̄ij = mean of specific cross i × j

8.6 Optimum Number of Test Environments

nopt = σGE / σG     // Ratio of GxE standard deviation to genetic SD; typically 3–8 environments

9. Advantages & Limitations

✅ Advantages

  • Increases frequency of favorable alleles systematically
  • Maintains genetic diversity — avoids genetic bottlenecks
  • Can exploit all types of gene action (depending on scheme)
  • Applicable to both cross- and self-pollinated crops
  • Provides improved parent material for line/hybrid development at any cycle
  • Theoretically unlimited progress if genetic variance is maintained
  • Compatible with molecular marker and genomic selection tools
  • Can simultaneously improve multiple traits with a selection index
  • Reduces linkage drag over cycles through recombination

❌ Limitations

  • Long cycle times (2–5 seasons per cycle) limit short-term gains
  • Expensive: large plot sizes, many crosses, multi-environment trials
  • Controlled pollinations required for some schemes
  • Inbreeding depression if Ne is too small
  • Progress may stall if genetic variance depleted (esp. small pops.)
  • G×E interactions complicate selection across environments
  • No fixed product (variety/hybrid) until extra development steps taken
  • Requires sophisticated statistical analysis and field infrastructure

11. Crop Applications

🌽 Maize (Zea mays)

The flagship crop for RS. RRS used to improve heterotic groups (Iowa Stiff Stalk Synthetic × Lancaster Sure Crop). BSSS, BS13, BS10 populations widely reported. Traits: grain yield, plant height, disease resistance, stalk quality.

🌾 Wheat (Triticum aestivum)

S₁ and full-sib RS used despite predominantly self-pollination. CIMMYT shuttle breeding incorporates RS principles. Targets: yield, rust resistance, quality parameters.

🌿 Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor)

RS used for grain yield, drought tolerance, stover quality, and disease resistance. Populations NP3R and SA populations are classic examples.

🫘 Soybean (Glycine max)

Despite autogamy, S₁ RS used for protein content, oil content, cyst nematode resistance, and iron deficiency chlorosis.

🌱 Rye (Secale cereale)

Cross-pollinated cereal; RS well-suited. Populations improved for grain yield, ergot resistance, and lodging resistance.

🌻 Sunflower (Helianthus annuus)

RS used for oil content, Sclerotinia resistance, Orobanche (broomrape) resistance, and yield components.

🥜 Groundnut (Arachis hypogaea)

Despite self-pollination, RS applied for aflatoxin resistance, drought tolerance, and pod yield. ICRISAT populations (ICGV) are examples.

🌾 Pearl Millet (Pennisetum glaucum)

Allogamous; RS widely used at ICRISAT. Populations improved for grain yield, downy mildew resistance, and stover quality in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

Forest Trees: RS is a cornerstone of tree improvement programs (loblolly pine, Eucalyptus, teak) where cycle length overlaps with rotation age. Genomic selection is revolutionizing these programs by shortening cycle time from decades to years.

12. Modern Advances

12.1 Marker-Assisted Recurrent Selection (MARS)

In MARS, molecular markers linked to QTLs of interest are used to pre-select individuals before field evaluation. This:

  • Increases selection accuracy, especially for low-heritability traits
  • Enables selection in off-season nurseries without phenotypic evaluation
  • Reduces cycle length by 30–50%
  • Allows simultaneous tracking of multiple traits

12.2 Genomic Selection (GS) in Recurrent Selection

Genomic selection (Meuwissen et al., 2001) uses genome-wide marker effects estimated from a training population to predict Genomic Estimated Breeding Values (GEBVs) for all selection candidates — even before field data is available.

GEBVi = Σ Zij · âj

// Zij = marker genotype matrix (coded 0,1,2)
// âj = estimated SNP effect for marker j (from rrBLUP, BayesB, etc.)

Integrating GS into RS allows cycle lengths to be reduced to a single season (or even shorter with controlled-environment facilities), dramatically increasing genetic gain per unit time.

12.3 Speed Breeding

Extended photoperiod, controlled temperature, and early seed harvest allow up to 4–6 crop cycles per year in glasshouses. Combined with GS, this can compress a 3-season RS cycle into a single calendar year.

12.4 High-Throughput Phenotyping (HTP)

UAV-based imaging, ground-penetrating radar, hyperspectral sensors, and LiDAR now provide rapid, high-resolution phenotypic data on thousands of plots. HTP reduces the bottleneck of phenotyping in large RS populations and improves accuracy of selection.

12.5 Double Haploid (DH) Integration

DHs can be generated from the best RS cycle families, instantly producing fully homozygous lines for variety or hybrid development — avoiding the 6–8 generation of selfing normally required. This "extract-and-fix" strategy links RS population improvement directly to commercial variety pipelines.

12.6 Multi-Trait Selection Indices

Rather than selecting on a single trait, selection indices combine multiple traits with economic weights derived from breeding goal equations. Modern implementations use genomic data to construct indices that improve several traits simultaneously without sacrificing individual trait heritability.

I = Σ bi · Pi     // bi = index weights (from H = Σ ai gi)
// Pi = phenotypic (or GEBV) values for each trait i

13. Notable Case Studies

13.1 Illinois Long-Term Selection Experiment (Maize Oil & Protein)

Initiated in 1896 by C. G. Hopkins at the University of Illinois. After 110+ generations:

  • High-Oil line: oil increased from 4.7% → >20%
  • Low-Oil line: oil reduced to <1%
  • High-Protein line: protein raised from 10.9% → ~27%
  • Low-Protein line: protein reduced to ~5%

This experiment conclusively demonstrates that quantitative traits have enormous reserves of additive genetic variance that respond to selection over many generations — the theoretical foundation of recurrent selection.

13.2 BSSS and BS10 Populations (Iowa State University)

BS13(S)C6 and BSSS(R)C7 populations (Hallauer et al.) showed consistent grain yield increases of ~2–4% per cycle through S₁ and full-sib RS. BSSS has been extensively used as parent material in commercial Iowa Stiff Stalk germplasm.

13.3 CIMMYT Maize Populations

CIMMYT's Tuxpeño and related populations underwent multiple cycles of RS for tropical lowland adaptation, Maize Streak Virus resistance, and drought tolerance. Documented gains of 90–120 kg/ha per cycle in target environments of sub-Saharan Africa.

13.4 ICRISAT Pearl Millet Populations

Population ICMV 221 and Iniadi-derived populations subjected to full-sib RS at ICRISAT (Hyderabad). After 5 cycles, grain yield improved by 8–12% and downy mildew incidence reduced by 40–60% in target environments (West Africa, South Asia).

13.5 Genomic Recurrent Selection in Barley (IPK Germany)

A genomic RS program in winter barley (2 cycles, each ~1 year using controlled environments) demonstrated genetic gains equivalent to 3–4 seasons of conventional RS. GEBVs using 17,000 SNPs were used for selection. Published by Massman et al. and extended by Longin et al.

Key Lesson from Case Studies: Genetic gain per unit time (ΔG/year), not just per cycle, is the critical metric. Modern tools (GS + speed breeding) can increase ΔG/year by 2–4× compared to conventional RS.
